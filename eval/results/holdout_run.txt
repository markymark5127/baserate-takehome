Q: What is JPMorgan's view on AI's impact on its expenses? 
A: JPMorgan's message is that AI will bring efficiency and will cost money, but it isn't a meaningful driver of expenses now, and management doesn't expect it to improve margins permanently.

- **Current expense impact is small.** On the 2Q26 call, CFO Jeremy Barnum said AI token expense is "a trivial number" in the first half. The company forecasts "some meaningful acceleration" in the second half, but the full-year contribution is still trivial and was partly budgeted. He said it is not a meaningful driver of the outlook or of the revision to it [1]. He called it a topic that may matter in the future. He also said JPMorgan will use "the right model for the right purpose" and be smart about open source to get value from the spend [2].
- **The guidance increase has other causes.** The 2026 adjusted expense outlook rose to about $107.5B. Barnum attributed the increase primarily to higher volume- and revenue-related expenses from activity levels and revenue outperformance [3]. Of the $2.5B increase, $1.5B is already booked against a $6.5B first-half capital markets outperformance [4].
- **Efficiency is expected, but the benefits go to customers.** Jamie Dimon said JPMorgan "fully expect[s]" huge efficiency in certain parts of the company. He said the company spends "quite a bit of money" on AI and has almost 1,000 use cases. In some discrete areas it has cut jobs by 30% or 40%, and most of those people were offered jobs elsewhere [5]. He also said competitors will do the same, so the benefit "accrues to the customer" and ROE won't "go to 50% and stay there" [6]. In 1Q26 he said it is "a bad idea" to expect AI to improve the efficiency ratio [7].
- **Operating leverage and 2027–28.** Dimon said AI "will have its gives and takes," so JPMorgan can't project the effect, and he said some spending is investment that shows up as expense in the short run. He suggested growth might slow in 2027 or 2028 [8].

Q: Which analyst is more bullish on Microsoft after FY27 Q1, and is their reasoning supported by the transcript? 
A: Joe Smith is the more bullish analyst. His efficiency argument is supported by the transcript, but one of his side claims is wrong.

**Who is more bullish.** Joe Smith's note says "My take: positive." He calls Azure re-accelerating toward 50% with near-50% operating margins "the cleanest evidence so far that the AI build-out is earning a return" [1]. Sally Barton's note says "neutral to positive." She would wait for proof that efficiency gains repeat before paying for 50% Azure growth [2]. Both notes are opinion.

**Where Smith is supported.**
- **Azure growth and efficiency.** Hood said Azure grew 48% despite third-party capacity delays. She said Foundry revenue more than tripled, helped by GPU efficiency gains and higher revenue per token. [3]
- **Q2 Azure guide.** Management guided Q2 Azure to 49–50% in constant currency, with continued CPU and GPU efficiency gains offsetting delayed third-party GPU contracts. [4]
- **Operating margin.** Hood said operating margin "increased slightly year-over-year to 50%." [5]

**Where Smith is wrong.** His note says "Microsoft has already started issuing debt." [6] Hood said the opposite on the call: Microsoft has access to the debt markets "and we haven't yet, I would say, tap that." [7]

**Caveat on the "return on capex" claim.** Management's own wording is more conditional than Smith's. Hood said the Q2 guide needs more efficiency gains and that token pricing is uncertain. [8] Barton's point that the guide is "less mechanical than usual" is therefore fair.

**Barton's reasoning.**
- Her claim that operating margin "slipped to about 45% from 50% a year ago" is contradicted by the transcript, which says margin rose slightly to 50% [5]. [9]
- Her cash-flow concern is supported. Capex was $45.2B, free cash flow was $18.6B, and Q2 capex is guided above $50B, or about $170B for the calendar year. [10]
- Her Cloud gross margin concern is also supported. It is guided to roughly 65%, down year over year on Azure mix. [11]

**Bottom line.** Smith's core thesis is backed by management's words. Barton's caution rests partly on a margin figure that is wrong, though her cash-flow and gross-margin points hold up.
